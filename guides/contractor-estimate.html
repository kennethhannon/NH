How to create a contractor estimate that survives a real job

Use line items, scope boundaries, quantities, tax assumptions, deposits and a simple review process.

Define the job before the price

A good estimate identifies the customer, project location, work areas, materials, and start assumptions. State exactly what is included and what is excluded, especially demolition, disposal, subfloor correction, moisture remediation, prep work and electrical work.

A vague line like 'finish the walls' can mean wildly different levels of sanding, patching, masking, priming, and painting. Describe the actual number of coats, materials, finish standard and areas covered so the customer knows what they are accepting.

Separate quantity, unit cost and extended cost

Break materials, labor and subcontractor tasks into clear line items with quantities and rates. For example, 12 hours at $65 per hour is $780 of labor before materials or overhead. Your internal margin depends on more than the worker's wage: insurance, vehicles, tools, travel, callbacks, and estimating time also cost money.

Use a separate line for unforeseen repairs rather than silently inflating another number. Flag items that are allowances or pending inspection. Keep the method understandable to a customer who is not a contractor.

Be careful with taxes and deposits

Construction taxes and required disclosures differ by jurisdiction and trade. An estimator can apply a tax percentage to selected items, but it cannot decide what is legally taxable. Verify local rules and explain what the percentage represents.

A deposit is not profit: it is part of the total, often collected before work begins. Spell out payment milestones and what happens if the project scope changes. The printed estimate is not automatically an enforceable signed contract.

Build a process for approvals and changes

Assign the estimate a date, validity period, customer name, and reference number. When a customer requests additional work, create a separate change-order price and obtain documented approval before proceeding.

Export a PDF for the customer and a backup of the source estimate for future editing. Test your PDF on a phone before relying on it in the field.
